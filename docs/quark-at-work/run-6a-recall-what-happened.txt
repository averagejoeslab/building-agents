**My recommendation is to keep the current order: Observability, Guardrails, Sandboxing, Resilience, Performance, Evaluation.** I partly disagree with Chase. I think his instinct about safety is sound, but his order costs more than it gains. I changed no files.

## Where I agree with Chase

- **The danger starts earlier than Lesson 5.** From Lesson 2 the agent runs model-written shell commands with no confirmation, and Lesson 5 only watches. A learner spends one more lesson with an unprotected agent that they can now see.
- **Sandboxing is the strongest control.** The kernel enforces it however a command is spelled, while a guardrail only reads text. Lesson 6 says as much in "What's missing".
- **Evaluation then Observability has one natural link.** Lesson 10 says an eval "tells you that something got worse, and doesn't say why: that's what Lesson 5's traces are for". Ending on "now find out why" is a reasonable capstone.

## Why I'd still keep Observability first

1. **It's the lowest-risk, smallest layer.** It adds only `trace()` calls and changes no behaviour. The exposure is the same as in Lessons 2–4, so putting it first adds almost no risk.
2. **Later layers use it.** The code is cumulative, and every later `quark.py` carries `trace()`.
   - Lesson 6's `guardrails.py` prices spend with "the same example rates as Lesson 5's".
   - Lesson 8's resilience README says retries "only show up when it reads the trace", and its `quark.py` logs `model_failed` events.
   - Lesson 9's cache savings are visible only through the `cache_read` and `cache_write` counts that Lesson 5 introduces.
   - Lesson 10's `quark.py` reads `.quark/traces.jsonl` for its steps, tokens and time. Placing Observability last would make the eval lesson depend on a lesson not yet taught.
3. **You can't judge a change you can't see.** Each later layer needs a way to confirm it did something: a denial, a kill at 137, a retry, a cache hit. Building five layers blind and adding instruments at the end means debugging them without data.

## Why Guardrails before Sandboxing

Chase puts Sandboxing first, and I'd keep it second to Guardrails for four reasons.

1. **Lesson 6's gap motivates Lesson 7.** Guardrails ends with "the check reads text… approval fatigue… what they can reach when they do [run]". Sandboxing then answers exactly that.
2. **They stack naturally.** Lesson 7's code keeps `guard()` running before the box. Taught the other way round, guardrails would read as a weaker add-on to a wall that already exists.
3. **Docker is a setup cost.** Lesson 7 is the first lesson that needs Docker, and Lessons 8–10's `quark.py` all contain `sandbox()`. Putting it first would force Docker onto the whole sequence, including Lessons 5 and 6, which don't need it.
4. **Guardrails covers more than the gate.** The step and token limits and the interrupt are plain control flow and work without a container.

## Why the tail stays Resilience, Performance, Evaluation

- **Resilience before Performance.** Lesson 9 leans on Lesson 8's backup model and the `max_retries` and timeout settings. It's referenced about ten times.
- **Performance before Evaluation.** Lesson 9 ends on the worry that every saving is a bet. Lesson 10 opens with that worry and measures it, and Lesson 10 is the only layer that wraps the whole harness.
- **Evaluation last.** It needs the finished set of layers to test.

## The case for Chase's order

If the goal is "safe to run unattended first", his order works. It would mean rewriting every `quark.py` from Lesson 5 on, and the READMEs' "What's missing" links, which chain 5→6→7→8→9→10.
